## Overview {#overview}

Oxytocin is the chemical of trust, love and friendship — the "warm and fuzzies". It makes us generous, empathic and social, and it works as a **social compass**: it tells us how vulnerable we can afford to be around someone. Unlike [dopamine]({{< relref "dopamine.md" >}}) it is slow and long-lasting; it builds with time spent together and leaves calm and safety rather than excitement. Sinek groups it with [serotonin]({{< relref "serotonin.md" >}}) as the "selfless" chemicals.


## How it is released {#how-it-is-released}

_([Leaders Eat Last: Why Some Teams Pull Together and Others Don't]({{< relref "../books/leaders_eat_last.md" >}}), Ch. 6)_

-   Doing something kind for someone, receiving kindness, or **merely witnessing** an act of generosity. This is why helping a stranger feels good and why stories of selfless acts inspire us ("paying it forward")
-   Physical contact: hugs, holding hands, handshakes, high-fives. Children deprived of touch have trouble forming trusting relationships later in life. A refused handshake at a deal signing makes both sides more nervous even if the contract is perfect
-   Time and shared vulnerability: the longer we know someone and the more trust is earned in both directions, the more it flows. Sinek's definition of love: giving someone the power to destroy us and trusting they won't use it
-   Belonging: the brain releases oxytocin in the presence of our tribe and [cortisol]({{< relref "cortisol.md" >}}) when we feel vulnerable and alone. Seeing a photo of people who love us is enough to feel less alone


## Effects {#effects}

_([Leaders Eat Last: Why Some Teams Pull Together and Others Don't]({{< relref "../books/leaders_eat_last.md" >}}), Ch. 6 and Ch. 7)_

-   Boosts the immune system, lowers blood pressure, relieves stress and counters cortisol — which in turn inhibits oxytocin
-   Improves problem solving and opens the mind; it makes us more resistant to the addictive pull of dopamine
-   Sinek cites a 2011 PNAS study (3,800 people aged 52–79) in which the happiest participants were far less likely to die in the following five years, and a 2012 Duke study finding that people who never marry are more than twice as likely to die in midlife


## Beyond work: relationships, addiction and hardship {#beyond-work-relationships-addiction-and-hardship}

_([Leaders Eat Last: Why Some Teams Pull Together and Others Don't]({{< relref "../books/leaders_eat_last.md" >}}), Ch. 25 and Ch. 26)_

-   Preliminary 2012 findings (UNC Chapel Hill) suggest oxytocin reduces withdrawal symptoms in alcoholics and heroin addicts. Sinek's example is Alcoholics Anonymous: meetings are held in person, people phone their sponsor rather than e-mail, and the final step is serving another alcoholic. Recovery works because the group is a real Circle of Safety, not a virtual one
-   Shared hardship bonds people: our fondest memories of work (or of school, a deployment, a bad trip) are the nights things went wrong and we got through it together, because struggle shared releases oxytocin. Soldiers often say they were "grateful" for a deployment they hated
-   Abundance has a cost: with less shared hardship there is less need to cooperate and so less oxytocin. We rarely help neighbours before the disaster, only after
-   Virtual relationships cannot substitute: likes and followers give a [dopamine]({{< relref "dopamine.md" >}}) hit with no sacrifice involved, whereas oxytocin requires time, presence and giving something up
