## Overview {#overview}

AI governance is the design of new institutions — domestic and international — capable of managing a technology that spreads faster and touches more parts of society than any prior general-purpose technology. The central claim is that none of today's institutions were built for this: AI simultaneously affects national security, employment, education, taxation, energy, elections, public health, the financial system, law enforcement, transportation, public lands, and IT systems, and it does so on a compressed timeline. Governance in this sense is not the same as [AI alignment]({{< relref "ai_alignment.md" >}}), which is about making individual models behave safely — governance is about the surrounding human institutions that decide how AI is deployed, taxed, restricted, and held accountable across an entire economy.


## The institutional design challenge {#the-institutional-design-challenge}

-   Existing agencies are siloed by function and see only one slice of the problem: a labor department understands workforce disruption but not security risk; a market regulator understands concentration but not AI's effects on children. Left alone, each institution manages its slice while the consequences of AI ripple across the whole system uncontained.
-   At the national level, new bodies are needed that can set cross-agency priorities so that no risk falls through the cracks between departments — the worry being that an AI-enabled attack or harm could succeed simply because no single agency considered it their job to stop it.
-   A country that gets its own house in order is still exposed to risks that cross borders, so a parallel international body is required — one built before, not after, a crisis forces governments into reactive mode.
-   Building this kind of framework is compared in scale to the U.S. government's reorganization after 9/11 (the biggest since World War II, for the single function of national security) — except AI touches far more functions at once.


## Precedents to draw from {#precedents-to-draw-from}

No existing institution is a full template, but partial precedents exist for pieces of the problem:

-   the nuclear weapons inspections regime, for verification of compliance across sovereign states
-   international aviation regulation, for coordinated technical safety standards enforced across borders
-   the agreements that protect the ozone layer (Montreal Protocol), for a global response to a diffuse, borderless harm with staged phase-outs

A new AI governance body is expected to need elements of all three, plus mechanisms none of them fully solve — e.g. verifying compliance for a technology, unlike nuclear material, that is difficult to physically inspect or contain.


## Who should be at the table {#who-should-be-at-the-table}

The case against leaving governance to AI companies themselves: some of the problems raised (labor markets, child development, elections) are outside their expertise, and in a democratic society it is not a private company's role to decide these trade-offs unilaterally. The proposed alternative is a public, democratic process involving elected officials, policymakers, educators, health workers, local officials, community leaders, workers, students entering the workforce, and religious and faith-based organizations — voices Gates argues are currently underrepresented in AI policy debates. He specifically cites Pope Leo XIV's 2026 encyclical **On Safeguarding the Human Person in the Time of Artificial Intelligence** as a notable contribution from religious leadership to this debate.
