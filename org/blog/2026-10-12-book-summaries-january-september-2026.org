As I sit here in the morning it feels so strange just to reflect on the books I've read this year already. It's not the
fact that I'm doing it, it's a recurring pattern I observe nowadays more often: *Thinking* (on your own) is *hard*! I could
just let some LLM summarize the whole books for you and write this blog post for you, right? But what's the point? I
mean, what would I gain from this? Training your intellect is still hard, doing deep work, ditto. But I guarantee you
it's for your own mental health.


That being said, this is a collection of book summaries for the books I read during ~January-September 2026~ categorized
by topic/genre.

* Philosophy
** 📚 How to think like Socrates
/Donald J. Robertson/ — [[https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/203578835-how-to-think-like-socrates][Goodreads]]

#+html: {{< gbox src="/posts/img/2026/book-summaries-january-september-2026/how-to-think-like-socrates-cover.png" title="How to think like Socrates" caption="" pos="left" >}}

After {{{zk_book(how-to-think-like-a-roman-emperor,How to think like a Roman Emperor)}}} I basically became a Robertson fan.

#+begin_quote
I know that I know nothing (Sokrates)
#+end_quote

This is one of the quotes my [[https://madincea.herder-oberschule.de/person.htm][mathematics teacher]] used to repeat at school. At that time I didn't know anything about
Socrates. A few years later, I finally understood where the *Socratic method* of asking questions comes from. He was known
for "asking a lot of questions" during his conversations, taking someone's confident definition (of something) and
testing it against cases until contradictions arose. The point was not to beat his opponent's arguments.
#+begin_sidenote
The Sophists, however, were known for doing everything to win a dispute. They were basically the "influencers" of Athens at that time.
#+end_sidenote
The point was that discovering you *don't know something* is indeed a virtue.

#+html: {{< notice info >}}
What is virtue? It comes from the Greek word /arete/. In our modern world people would translate it to "excellence" and refer to the qualities that make someone exceptionally good. 
#+html: {{< /notice >}}


Socrates was sentenced to death for /impiety/ (not recognizing the gods of the city and introducing new ones) and
for /corrupting the youth/ of Athens (mainly because he taught them how to think and question everything). Long before
that, Socrates' friend /Chaerephon/ had asked the /Oracle of Delphi/ whether anyone was wiser than Socrates. The answer was:
"no one is wiser". Socrates couldn't believe it, so he treated the oracle's answer as something to be /tested/ and
"interrogated" politicians, poets and craftsmen in order to find someone wiser. He came to the conclusion that the
oracle was right: he was indeed the wisest person, but only in this narrow sense: /he alone did not think he knew what he
did not know/.

The link to {{{zk_tag(stoicism,Stoicism)}}} is direct: *Zeno of Citium* came to philosophy after reading about Socrates.

/Marcus Aurelius/ — [[https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/233411514-meditations][Goodreads]]

#+html: {{< gbox src="/posts/img/2026/book-summaries-january-september-2026/meditations-marcus-aurelius.png" title="Meditații (Romanian edition)" caption="" pos="left" >}}

And it was a *huge disappointment*. Let me explain.


Reading it after the secondary literature was the right thing for me. Robertson's book gave me the biographical
background and made me curious to read more about Marcus Aurelius in the first place.
#+begin_sidenote
Read my notes about [[/2026/02/27/book-summaries-april-december-2025/#how-to-think-like-a-roman-emperor][How to think like a Roman emperor]]

* Politics
** 📚 Blueprint for Revolution
/Srdja Popović/ — [[https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/22609404-blueprint-for-revolution][Goodreads]]

#+html: {{< gbox src="/posts/img/2026/book-summaries-january-september-2026/blueprint-for-revolution-cover.png" title="Blueprint for Revolution" caption="" pos="left" >}}

Full subtitle: /"How to Use Rice Pudding, Lego Men, and Other Nonviolent Techniques to Galvanize Communities, Overthrow
Dictators, and Save the World"/. Which was appealing enough.

Popović was one of the founders of *Otpor!*, the Serbian student movement that played a central role in bringing
down [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milosevic][Milošević]] in 2000. He later went on to train activists elsewhere through CANVAS.

The central claim is that successful *nonviolent* movements are *built, not spontaneous*, and that they follow certain
patterns:

- Every revolution has to be prepared (years!) in advance before taking place. Winning small fights first was just as
  important as finding the right people.

  #+begin_quote
  Proper revolutions are not cataclysmic explosions; they are long, controlled burns.
  #+end_quote

- You don't fight the dictator! You have to shake his /pillars/. No regime is self-sufficient. You need ordinary people
  who are loyal enough to execute the orders. Popović's strategy was to peel away those "pillars" (e.g. pressuring
  foreign companies to pull their money out of the country, scaring tourists away from visiting the country) rather than confronting the
  regime directly.

- Make fun of the regime! Facing Milošević's secret police, activists painted his face on a *barrel*, put it on a busy
  street with a bat, and let people take turns hitting it for a coin. When police showed up, they had two options:
  arrest ordinary shoppers and look like thugs, or arrest a barrel and look ridiculous. They arrested the barrel. It
  made front pages.

- Unite the opposition. At that time Serbia's opposition parties had fought and fractured for years, which is exactly why
  Milošević could stay in power for so long. Otpor's contribution before the 2000 election was to force the
  opposition leaders to unite behind a single candidate, under one coalition. This way all parties (despite their
  political programs and ideologies) had the same goal: Bring Milošević down! If the opposition hadn't agreed on
  this, all the preparation and activism would have been wasted (because the vote would have split and the regime would have won
  by default).

  #+begin_quote
  Parties are the guardians of democracy.
  #+end_quote

  (Quote from {{{zk_book(how-democracies-die,How Democracies Die)}}}, Levitsky & Ziblatt. Parties act as gatekeepers /before/ an
  authoritarian takes power, screening out extremist candidates. Otpor showed the other half of that logic. /After/ an
  authoritarian is already in power, opposition parties have to unite behind one candidate to remove him.)

#+html: {{< notice info >}}
*Where has CANVAS been active?* Popović's CANVAS has trained activists in more than 50 countries since 2004, including
Iran, Zimbabwe, Burma, Venezuela, Ukraine, Georgia, Belarus, Azerbaijan, Tunisia and Egypt.
#+html: {{< /notice >}}

Hungary is a recent case. CANVAS published an analysis of Tisza's landslide win over Orbán in the April 2026
parliamentary election, tracing Péter Magyar's path from inside Fidesz to leading the movement that ended Orbán's
16-year rule (Tisza won 141 of 199 seats on 53.6% of the vote, a two-thirds supermajority). Check out [[https://canvasopedia.org/][canvasopedia.org]]
for further details.
** 📚 Earth4All
/Sandrine Dixson-Declève, Owen Gaffney, Jayati Ghosh, Jørgen Randers, Johan Rockström & Per Espen Stoknes/ — [[https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/60878390-earth-for-all][Goodreads]]
• {{{zk_book(earth4all,Notes)}}}

#+html: {{< gbox src="/posts/img/2026/book-summaries-january-september-2026/earth4all-cover.png" title="Earth for All" caption="" pos="left" >}}


The Earth4All model is the successor of World3, the model behind {{{zk_topic(limits-to-growth,The Limits to Growth)}}}
(1972). Jørgen Randers worked on both. What the new model adds:

- *Ten regions* instead of one global average, so growth in one part of the world no longer hides stagnation in another
- *Inequality* is modelled explicitly, which World3 didn't do
- Two new indicators, the *Average Wellbeing Index* and the *Social Tension Index*. When wellbeing declines, tension
  rises, trust erodes and governments lose the ability to act
- Calibrated against real data from 1980 to 2020, then run forward to 2100

This book outlines 2 scenarios which, in the context of today's global crises (climate breakdown, rising
inequality, eroding trust in institutions), might have a big impact on humanity:

- *"Too Little Too Late"*

  #+begin_quote
  Scenario 1: Too Little Too Late. This scenario shows the potential consequences of continuing world development along
  the same dynamics as 1980 to 2020. The overall global result is a somewhat slowing population growth and world economic
  growth to 2050 and beyond, but also declining labor participation rates, declining trust in government, a steady
  increase in the ecological footprint, and rising loss in biodiversity.
  #+end_quote
  
  This is definitely not a do-nothing scenario. This scenario implies that the
  world leaders don't take enough action to combat all the challenges humanity
  is facing. There are some actions but these are slow:
  
  - /some/ climate action
  - /some/ social spending
  - people trust in /market self-regulation/
    
  The result: temperatures overshoot well past 1.5°C (heading toward 2.5°C by
  2100), inequality keeps widening, and social tensions keep rising. All
  this triggers more frequent economic and ecological /shocks/ that make
  coordinated action even harder going forward.

- *"Giant Leap"*

  #+begin_quote
  As our second scenario, we chose Giant Leap, which illustrates the effects of the powerful and immediate
  implementation of the five extraordinary policy turnarounds. Its passage through the century is driven not by
  tinkering at the fringes but by fundamentally reconfiguring economies, energy systems, and food systems. It's a major
  upgrade. A reset. An essential reboot of civilization's guiding rules before the system crashes. Due to inertia in
  economies and in the climate, the main impacts of any action taken today are often not seen for years in economies and
  decades or centuries in the climate. We believe that nothing less than a "giant leap" now is needed if humanity is
  serious about turning around from the current trajectory and getting on a new track to a sustainable world by 2050.
  #+end_quote
  
  Five simultaneous policy turnarounds, each quantified rather than aspirational:

  | Turnaround           | Target                                                                                                              |
  |----------------------+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
  | Poverty              | ≥5%/yr growth for low-income countries until >$15,000 per person, plus reform of the international financial system |
  | Inequality           | the richest 10% take less than 40% of national income                                                               |
  | Empowerment of women | gender equity plus education and health investment, stabilising global population below 9 billion by 2050           |
  | Food                 | a regenerative, nature-positive food system; no net cropland expansion                                              |
  | Energy               | halve emissions roughly every decade to net-zero by 2050                                                            |

The part I keep thinking about is the *Citizens Fund* / *Universal Basic Dividend* (UBD) proposal:

| Element   | What the book proposes                                                                                                                                                              |
|-----------+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Fee       | companies pay for extracting or using shared commons (fossil fuels, land, freshwater, the ocean, minerals, the atmosphere, even data and knowledge)                                 |
| Fund      | the fees go into a national Citizens Fund                                                                                                                                           |
| Dividend  | the revenue is paid out equally to every citizen. It is deliberately not limited to the poorest, because the authors argue the middle class only supports a policy it benefits from |
| Purpose   | economic security during the transformation, which the authors call an "innovation net" rather than just a safety net                                                               |
| Precedent | the Alaska Permanent Fund has paid every resident a share of the oil revenues since 1976, typically $1,000 to $2,000 per year                                                       |

Is this the same as Universal Basic Income? Not quite. UBI describes the payout (a regular income regardless of working
status) and leaves open who pays for it. The UBD is defined by its source: it is a share of what the commons earn, not a
transfer financed by taxes. The book treats the two as close relatives and speaks of "providing universal basic income
through a *Citizens Fund*".

** 📚 Reclaiming Conversation
Sherry Turkle — [[https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/24612127-reclaiming-conversation][Goodreads]] • {{{zk_book(reclaiming-conversation,Notes)}}}

#+html: {{< gbox src="/posts/img/2026/book-summaries-january-september-2026/reclaiming-conversation-cover.png" title="Reclaiming Conversation" caption="" pos="left" >}}

This is a book that is a combination of {{{zk_book(digital-minimalism,Digital Minimalism)}}}
and {{{zk_book(the-anxious-generation,The Anxious Generation)}}}. I've found Haidt very empirical whereas Turkle was somehow
ethnographic: years of interviews in families, schools and workplaces.

The book is organised around *Thoreau's three chairs*. Thoreau kept exactly three chairs in his
Walden cabin:
- one for *solitude*
- one for *friendship*
- one for *society*

Turkle uses this as a map of what smartphones destroy: first our inner life, then our closest relationships,
then our public life. She adds a fourth chair of her own: what happens when we talk to machines.

- Even a silent phone degrades the conversation.

  Face-down, switched off, doesn't matter. The fact it's just /there/ lowers connection (in that moment) and empathy with
  the conversation partners. By anticipating interruption (why did we put the phone there at all?) we prefer light
  topics instead of /deep/, focused conversations.
  
- Solitude is a skill, not a state.

  The definition I already knew comes from {{{zk_book(digital-minimalism,Digital Minimalism)}}}, where Cal Newport
  borrows it from /Lead Yourself First/ (Kethledge & Erwin):

  #+begin_quote
  {{{zk_topic(solitude,solitude)}}} is a subjective state in which your mind is /free from input from other minds/. It's about
  what happens in your brain, not about the environment around you. You can be in solitude in a crowded café and miss it
  completely alone in your room, scrolling.
  #+end_quote

  Turkle separates two things we often mix up (the distinction comes from /Paul Tillich/):

  - /Loneliness/ is the pain of being alone
  - /Solitude/ is the "glory" of being alone

  We learn solitude as children through good relationships with other people. If we never learn it, being alone
  always feels like loneliness (so we reach for the phone). And that's exactly the mantra of the book: *constant connection makes
  us lonelier*.
  
- Boredom is good.

  We're bored because we're used to a constant influx of information. We need space and time for our mind to wander
  and come up with new ideas. Johann Hari describes this in {{{zk_book(stolen-focus,Stolen Focus)}}}: when we seem to be
  doing nothing, the brain's /default mode network/ becomes active. A wandering mind makes new connections between
  things it already knows. And that's where new ideas come from.
  
- "I share, therefore I am."

  #+begin_quote
  And, more than a symptom, constant connection is changing the way people think of themselves. It is shaping a new way
  of being. I call it "I share, therefore I am." We share our thoughts and feelings in order to feel whole.
  #+end_quote
  
  I think this is nothing new and we all know it: Folks who have to share every single moment of their life as if the
  whole world watches them.

  But Turkle means something deeper than showing off:

  - /Before/: you have a feeling, you deal with it on your own, and then (maybe) you tell someone about it
  - /Now/: you have to share the feeling in order to feel it at all

  Turkle puts it like this:

  #+begin_quote
  /"I want to have a feeling; I need to send a text."/
  #+end_quote

  People who grew up with social media told her they can't feel like themselves unless they are posting or messaging.
  Sharing is no longer what you do /after/ an experience. It has become part of the experience: without the post, it
  doesn't feel real.
  
- We are vulnerable, not addicted.

  Turkle doesn't like calling our phone use an "addiction". The word matters, because it decides what the solution
  looks like:

  
  | Word       | What it means for the solution                                                                                                                                    |
  |------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
  | Addicted   | the only way out is to quit completely. But nobody can realistically live without a phone.                                                                        |
  | Vulnerable | the phone is built to exploit our weak spots (boredom, loneliness, the fear of missing out). Once we know where these are, we can protect them and keep the phone |

  #+begin_quote
  If you are addicted, you have to get off your drug. If you are vulnerable, you can work to be less vulnerable.
  #+end_quote

  I find this much more useful in practice.

The virtuous circle she describes is the thesis in one line:

#+begin_example
solitude → self-knowledge → empathy → richer conversation → deeper solitude
#+end_example

Break it at any point and the whole thing degrades.

** 📚 Software Engineering at Google
Titus Winters, Tom Manshreck & Hyrum Wright — [[https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/48816586-software-engineering-at-google][Goodreads]] • {{{zk_book(software-engineering-at-google,Notes)}}}

#+html: {{< gbox src="/posts/img/2026/book-summaries-january-september-2026/software-engineering-at-google-cover.png" title="Software Engineering at Google" caption="" pos="left" >}}

What is Software Engineering about nowadays? It used to be a discipline which took years (decades!) to master, where more
and more sophisticated software was "engineered" to deal with complex topics. But what about today? Every dev is now a
senior, every dev is now a (software) architect.

From the book (definition of "coding"):

#+begin_quote
Programming is certainly a significant part of software engineering: after all, programming is how you generate new
software in the first place. If you accept this distinction, it also becomes clear that we might need to delineate
between programming tasks (development) and software engineering tasks (development, modification, maintenance). The
addition of time adds an important new dimension to programming. Cubes aren't squares, distance isn't velocity. Software
engineering isn't programming.
#+end_quote

Definition of "software engineering":

#+begin_quote
We propose that "software engineering" encompasses not just the act of writing code, but all of the tools and processes
an organization uses to build and maintain that code over time.
#+end_quote

So, software engineering is way more than just ... writing code. Writing the actual code is just one step. Thinking
about the problem, modeling it, thinking about different solutions/aspects, coming up with a PoC, implementing, testing,
deploying it, maintaining it, incorporating customer feedback. All this is what software engineering is about.

From a technical point of view, Google has standardized quite a lot for its teams:


| Area                                                                                  | What every team at Google uses                                                                                                                                  |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Version control                                                                       | one monorepo and {{{zk_topic(trunk-based-development,trunk-based development)}}}: no long-lived branches, all commits go to head                                |
| Dependencies                                                                          | the One-Version Rule: nobody gets to choose "which version of this component should I depend on?"                                                               |
| Build system                                                                          | one artifact-based build system (Blaze, open-sourced as Bazel), external dependencies pinned in source control                                                  |
| Style                                                                                 | one style guide per language, enforced by formatters and linters wherever possible                                                                              |
| Code review                                                                           | every change needs three approvals: correctness (any engineer), readability (language-certified) and owner (OWNERS file)                                        |
| Testing and CI                                                                        | a test pyramid of roughly 80% unit, 15% integration and 5% end-to-end tests, one {{{zk_topic(continuous-integration,CI)}}} system (TAP) validating every commit |
| {{{zk_topic(lessons-from-building-static-analysis-tools-at-google,Static analysis)}}} | runs inside the code review tool (Critique), not only in CI                                                                                                     |
| Code Search                                                                           | one fast search over all the code                                                                                                                               |
| Large-scale changes                                                                   | automated tooling for changes touching thousands of files, such as an API rename across the whole codebase                                                      |
| Compute                                                                               | Borg, the ancestor of {{{zk_topic(kubernetes,Kubernetes)}}}: declare the desired state and the scheduler places the job                                         |


Apart from the technical aspects, I've enjoyed the /cultural/ ones even more. What is leadership about? How to deal with
superstars and geniuses? How to lead a team?

- The /Genius Myth/.

  We romanticize lone geniuses (Linus, Guido, Gates), but success is always a team effort. Working alone in your "cave"
  only delays the moment somebody spots your design mistakes. It also keeps the /bus factor/ low: the number of people
  who need to be hit by a bus before the project is doomed.

- {{{zk_topic(psychological-safety,Psychological safety)}}} comes first.

  It's the most critical factor for an effective team, backed by Google's own research. If people are afraid of making
  mistakes, they stop asking questions and stop being transparent. The result: /information islands/, /single points of
  failure/ and /haunted graveyards/ (code nobody dares to touch).

- Jerks are not good leaders.

  This is the answer to the superstar question. Expertise and kindness are not mutually exclusive, and a "brilliant jerk"
  is harmful to knowledge sharing no matter how good the code is.

  #+begin_quote
  Leaders improve the quality of the people around them, improve the team's psychological safety, create a culture of
  teamwork and collaboration, defuse tensions within the team, set an example of Google's culture and values, and make
  Google a more vibrant and exciting place to work. Jerks are not good leaders.
  #+end_quote

- Resist the urge to manage.

  Leadership here means /servant leadership/: remove obstacles, build consensus, shield the team from the chaos above.
  See also {{{zk_topic(engineering-management,engineering management)}}}.

  #+begin_quote
  Traditional managers worry about how to get things done, whereas great managers worry about what things get done (and
  trust their team to figure out how to do it).
  #+end_quote

- /Always be leaving/.

  The goal is a team that solves the problem by itself, without you. If you can't take a week off without checking
  email, you've made yourself a single point of failure.

I would definitely recommend reading this book, not necessarily for the practical /technical/ advice it includes. I think
the cultural aspects are something every big corp should incorporate.

** 📚 Team Topologies
Matthew Skelton & Manuel Pais — [[https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/44135420-team-topologies][Goodreads]]

#+html: {{< gbox src="/posts/img/2026/book-summaries-january-september-2026/team-topologies-cover.png" title="Team Topologies" caption="" pos="left" >}}

After reading {{{zk_book(accelerate,Accelerate)}}} I definitely wanted to read more about this topic. However, it took more
than 4 years to finally read this book.

The starting point is *{{{zk_topic(conways-law,Conway's Law)}}}*: organisations produce designs that mirror their own
communication structures. Skelton and Pais come up with the "inverse Conway manoeuvre", where you deliberately shape
teams to produce the architecture you want.

From there: four team types (*stream-aligned*, *enabling*, *complicated-subsystem*, *platform*) and
three interaction modes (*collaboration*, *X-as-a-Service*, *facilitating*). Most teams should be
stream-aligned; everything else exists to reduce the *cognitive load* on those teams.

Team types:

- Stream-aligned

  #+begin_quote
  A stream-aligned team is a team aligned to a single, valuable stream of work; this might be a single product or
  service, a single set of features, a single user journey, or a single user persona.
  #+end_quote

- Enabling

  #+begin_quote
  The end goal of an enabling team is to increase the autonomy of stream-aligned teams by growing their capabilities
  with a focus on their problems first, not the solutions per se.
  #+end_quote

- Complicated-subsystem

  #+begin_quote
  A complicated-subsystem team is responsible for building and maintaining a part of the system that depends heavily on
  specialist knowledge, to the extent that most team members must be specialists in that area of knowledge in order to
  understand and make changes to the subsystem.
  #+end_quote

- Platform

  #+begin_quote
  The purpose of a platform team is to enable stream-aligned teams to deliver work with substantial autonomy. The
  stream-aligned team maintains full ownership of building, running, and fixing their application in production.
  #+end_quote

Interaction modes:
- Collaboration: working closely together with another team
- X-as-a-Service: consuming or providing something with minimal collaboration (to another team)
- Facilitating: helping (or being helped by) another team to clear impediments


Besides Conway's Law I've found the concept of /cognitive load/ something that most organizations don't think about.
Whenever you put individuals into teams, you should draw clear team boundaries as well: What is within the
team's responsibility and what is not. That should not be decided from above or according to some org chart. When a team owns
more than it can hold in its /collective/ head, symptoms such as slow delivery, poor software quality and long processes
should not be ignored. Reorganising the people won't fix it if you don't reduce the cognitive load that team has to handle.

It reads well alongside {{{zk_book(software-engineering-at-google,Software Engineering at Google)}}}. Google's answer to
cognitive load is enormous investment in tooling and platform teams, which is essentially the Team Topologies platform
pattern with a very large budget behind it.

Well, you should know about Matt Ruff. He's a famous SciFi author.

So, what is the story about? John Chu is a *"sherpa"*, a paid guide who walks rich clients through online role-playing

As I'm writing this last chapter I realize I've spent more than 3 hours putting down thoughts
