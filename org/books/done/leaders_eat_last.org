* About this book

Humans are social animals whose behaviour is steered by a handful of chemicals, and leaders — in any group, with or without rank — are the people who create or destroy the conditions in which those chemicals reward cooperation. Sinek's central image is the *Circle of Safety*: a perimeter of trust that leaders build by putting the people in their care before themselves ("leaders eat last"). When people feel safe inside it they cooperate and look out for each other; when they don't, [[id:b57f02f1-5f51-4426-a6e4-c3cf721c9cd5][cortisol]] makes everyone guard themselves.

The wiki ingest follows two threads: the chemicals themselves, and what leadership means outside management and work — parenting, friendship, adolescence, addiction recovery and shared struggle.

* Key concepts

- [[id:41898058-fd6b-48c5-bbbd-513625f4e3b2][Leadership]] — leadership as a choice to look after the people in your care; the cost of leadership; leadership as parenting; service as the way out of addiction
- [[id:b561773c-b424-4366-97ff-a08da298f90f][Dopamine]] — chemical of progress and instant gratification; tangible goals; addictions to alcohol, gambling, phones and social media
- [[id:c14c7068-96d8-41be-8033-67cbe15cb448][Endorphins]] — pain-masking and endurance; runner's high; laughter
- [[id:2b6e8820-a254-4138-ad80-dc71c97a8082][Serotonin]] — pride, status and responsibility towards those we lead or are led by
- [[id:cacce695-0420-4ecb-8ea4-77d8fa31de28][Oxytocin]] — trust, love and belonging; touch and generosity; its role in stress, immunity, addiction recovery and shared hardship
- [[id:b57f02f1-5f51-4426-a6e4-c3cf721c9cd5][Cortisol]] — stress and the early-warning system; health costs; work-life balance as "where we feel safe"
- [[id:27fc80ff-3162-43aa-9e83-22c6c36e1793][Psychological safety]] — the team-level counterpart of the Circle of Safety
- [[id:5df3ff79-9b51-4bcc-8315-3c8efcff565f][Attention economy]] — notifications and likes as dopamine triggers

* Quotes

** Leadership as care
#+begin_quote
“If your actions inspire others to dream more, learn more, do more and become more, you are a leader.”
#+end_quote
- Foreword. John Quincy Adams, quoted by the foreword's author as the essence of the book
- Defines leadership by its effect on others, not by rank

#+begin_quote
Everything about being a leader is like being a parent. It is about committing to the well-being of those in our care and having a willingness to make sacrifices to see their interests advanced so that they may carry our banner long after we are gone.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 27 (We Need More Leaders). Sinek's closing argument that leadership is a commitment to people, comparable to parenting
- Echoes Bob Chapman's wedding story in Ch. 2: employers are handed someone's child to look after

#+begin_quote
Empathy is, as Johnny Bravo explains, “a second by second, minute by minute service that [we] owe to everyone if [we] want to call [ourselves] a leader.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 27. Johnny Bravo is the A-10 pilot from the book's opening story; he snapped at his tired crew chief, who then explained he was up at night with a new baby
- Point: empathy is owed to the people around you at every moment, not only to customers or those you formally serve

#+begin_quote
As Bob Chapman, CEO of Barry-Wehmiller, is fond of saying, “No one wakes up in the morning to go to work with the hope that someone will manage us. We wake up in the morning and go to work with the hope that someone will lead us.” The problem is, for us to be led, there must be leaders we want to follow.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 12 (The Boomers All Grown Up). Chapman is the Barry-Wehmiller CEO whose company used shared furloughs instead of layoffs in 2008
- Contrasts managing (metrics, resources) with leading (people)

#+begin_quote
Chapman and others like him didn’t set out to change their employees—they set out to change the conditions in which their employees operate. To create cultures that inspire people to give all they have to give simply because they love where they work.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 2 (Employees Are People Too). Chapman's approach: fix the environment, not the people
- Same idea as the Circle of Safety: leaders shape the conditions in which the chemicals reward trust and cooperation

#+begin_quote
Truly human leadership protects an organization from the internal rivalries that can shatter a culture. When we have to protect ourselves from each other, the whole organization suffers. But when trust and cooperation thrive internally, we pull together and the organization grows stronger as a result.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 2. Summary of what the Circle of Safety is for: protection from threats inside the group, so energy goes to the outside challenges
- Links to [[id:b57f02f1-5f51-4426-a6e4-c3cf721c9cd5][cortisol]] (self-protection mode) and [[id:cacce695-0420-4ecb-8ea4-77d8fa31de28][oxytocin]] (trust and cooperation)

** Hierarchy and the leader's job
#+begin_quote
As much as we all like the idea of being equal, the fact is we are not and never will be . . . and for good reason. Without some rules of order, when the hunters brought back the fresh kill to the tribe, everyone would rush in to eat. There would be a lot of pushing and shoving. Invariably, the ones who were lucky enough to be built like linebackers would get to eat first, whereas the “the artist of the family” would consistently get shoved aside or hurt. This is not a very good system if Mother Nature is trying to keep the species alive. The ones who were shoved aside would likely be less willing to trust or work closely with someone who had punched them earlier that afternoon. So to solve the problem, we evolved into hierarchical animals.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 8 (Why We Have Leaders). Sinek's evolutionary origin of hierarchy: we defer to the alpha, who eats first, and expect protection in return
- This is the "social contract" behind the title; see [[id:2b6e8820-a254-4138-ad80-dc71c97a8082][serotonin]] (status) and [[id:41898058-fd6b-48c5-bbbd-513625f4e3b2][Leadership]]

#+begin_quote
It is a leader’s job instead to take responsibility for the success of each member of his crew. It is the leader’s job to ensure that they are well trained and feel confident to perform their duties.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 18 (So Goes the Leader, so Goes the Culture). About [[https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/16158601-turn-the-ship-around][Captain David Marquet]], who took over the submarine USS Santa Fe
- Contrasts the leader as order-giver with the leader as the person responsible for his people's success

#+begin_quote
“The goal of a leader is to give no orders,” Captain Marquet explains. “Leaders are to provide direction and intent and allow others to figure out what to do and how to get there.” And this is the challenge most organizations face. “We train people to comply, not to think,” Captain Marquet goes on. If people only comply, we can’t expect people to take responsibility for their actions. The chain of command is for orders, not information. Responsibility is not doing as we are told, that’s obedience. Responsibility is doing what is right.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 18. Marquet's principle of giving intent rather than orders; compliance is not the same as responsibility
- Illustrated in the book by an order ("ahead two thirds") that nobody questioned although the new submarine had no such setting

#+begin_quote
“Those at the top,” explains Captain Marquet, “have all the authority and none of the information. Those at the bottom,” he continues, “have all the information and none of the authority. Not until those without information relinquish their control can an organization run better, smoother and faster and reach its maximum potential.” The problem, Captain Marquet says, was that he was “addicted” to being in control. And the crew, like in so many organizations that follow a flawed interpretation of hierarchy, were trained for compliance. In organizations in which few take responsibility for their own actions, at some point something bad is going to happen. Something bad that was probably highly preventable.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 18. Marquet on the information/authority mismatch in hierarchies, and on his own addiction to control
- Control as a kind of [[id:b561773c-b424-4366-97ff-a08da298f90f][dopamine]]-style addiction fits the book's wider addiction theme

#+begin_quote
In his book, Turn the Ship Around!, Captain Marquet goes through all the specific steps he took—that any organization can take—to develop an environment in which those who know more, the people who are actually doing the work, are empowered to make decisions.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 18. Pointer to Marquet's own book ([[https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/16158601-turn-the-ship-around][/Turn the Ship Around!]]/) for the practical steps

** Purpose, struggle and abundance
#+begin_quote
In the case of our businesses, neither our bosses nor our clients are the ultimate authorities over us. And in the case of public companies, nor are the shareholders or Wall Street analysts. And believe it or not, a small company does not answer ultimately to its investors either. All these “authorities” are Milgram’s scientists in white lab coats. Authorities in the situation, perhaps, but not ultimate authorities over the decisions we make. Just as Milgram’s study would have us predict, those leaders and companies with a strong sense of why, a strong sense of purpose and courage enough to stand up to the pressure of Wall Street or abstract shareholders are the ones that do better in the long term.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 26 (Shared Struggle), section "The Value of Purpose". Uses Milgram's obedience experiment: people who believed in a higher purpose than the scientist's authority refused to harm others
- Examples given: Bob Chapman (Barry-Wehmiller) and Jim Sinegal (Costco), who answer to a cause rather than to short-term numbers

#+begin_quote
To really inspire us, we need a challenge that outsizes the resources available. We need a vision of the world that does not yet exist. A reason to come to work. Not just a big goal to achieve. This is what leaders of great organizations do. They frame the challenge in terms so daunting that literally no one yet knows what to do or how to solve it.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 26, section "Redefining the Struggle". Large, resource-rich organizations lose the innovation of small ones unless leaders set a challenge bigger than their resources
- Examples: Bill Gates's PC on every desk, Steve Jobs's "dent in the universe"

#+begin_quote
Abundance destroys value.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 26. Commoditization: when something becomes ubiquitous we stop appreciating it (computers are the example); what we work hard for is what we value
- Related: shared hardship releases [[id:cacce695-0420-4ecb-8ea4-77d8fa31de28][oxytocin]], so abundance weakens the pull to cooperate

#+begin_quote
Human beings have thrived for fifty thousand years not because we are driven to serve ourselves, but because we are inspired to serve others. That’s the value of Step Twelve. All we need are leaders to give us a good reason to commit ourselves to each other.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 26, end of the chapter. Summary of the purpose argument and a link back to AA's Step Twelve from Ch. 25

** Environment, control and stress
#+begin_quote
Too many of the environments in which we work today frustrate our natural inclinations to trust and cooperate.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 12. Part of Sinek's argument that our biology (cooperation, sharing) is out of step with how modern workplaces are organised

#+begin_quote
It is not the demands of the job that cause the most stress, but the degree of control workers feel they have throughout their day. The studies also found that the effort required by a job is not in itself stressful, but rather the imbalance between the effort we give and the reward we feel. Put simply: less control, more stress.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 4 (Yeah, but . . .). The Whitehall Studies of British civil servants, relating rank to stress
- Finding: stress comes from lack of control and from an imbalance between effort and reward, not from responsibility; background for [[id:b57f02f1-5f51-4426-a6e4-c3cf721c9cd5][cortisol]]

** Scale and culture
#+begin_quote
Though Bill Gore was trusting his gut based on his own observations, it’s no coincidence that he arrived at the 150 person limit. Robin Dunbar, British anthropologist and a professor in the Department of Experimental Psychology at Oxford University, arrived at this same conclusion. Professor Dunbar figured out that people simply cannot maintain more than about 150 close relationships. “Putting it another way,” he likes to say, “it’s the number of people you would not feel embarrassed about joining uninvited for a drink if you happened to bump into them in a bar.

** Shareholder value
#+begin_quote
Though Milton Friedman first proposed the general idea, it was a pair of academics—William Meckling, of the University of Rochester, and Michael Jensen, of the Harvard Business School—who spread it with a piece they published in the Journal of Financial Economics in 1976. It was the answer everyone was looking for, a formula that could solve the problems of a corporate America fed up with stagnation and falling profits.
In 2012, Professor Lynn Stout of Cornell Law School wrote a definitive work on the subject, The Shareholder Value Myth, in which she points out that shareholder value was instantly appealing to two influential groups: activist corporate raiders and company CEOs, the very groups that would stand to benefit most from it. And so it took hold. Carl Icahn and other corporate raiders began trolling for financially stricken companies to swallow (and there were a lot of them in those days). They typically went looking for companies whose stock was undervalued, bought up shares and then forced the board to cut expenses, usually through layoffs or by selling parts of the company. At the same time, the pay of corporate executives became directly linked to stock performance in the form of options and bonuses, thereby ensuring that executives were financially incentivized to put their priorities ahead of both customers and employees.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 21 (Lead the People, Not the Numbers). History of the "maximize shareholder value" idea: Friedman, then Meckling and Jensen (1976), then raiders and stock-linked executive pay
- Drawn from Lynn Stout's /The Shareholder Value Myth/ (2012)

#+begin_quote
It seems reasonable that the leaders of companies should work hard to protect the interests of the companies’ owners. However, a strong case can be made that the shareholders don’t actually own the companies. In the view of Professor Stout, Friedman, the hero of modern capitalist economics, was simply wrong. There is no legal standing to the idea that shareholders are the true owners of corporations. They simply own shares, which are abstract representations. In legalese, corporations own themselves. And given that shareholders are not the true owners of corporations, corporations have no legal requirement to maximize share price, as many have claimed.
Professor Stout takes this thinking even further to argue that maximizing shareholder value has failed. Sure it has fattened the pockets of the corporate elite, but in virtually every other way it has been bad for business and bad for the companies themselves. Employees are forced to work in atmospheres where short-term performance is valued above all else and where the well-being of people is almost always put second. The consequences of which are empirically bad for the company. And contrary to its claim, shareholder value maximization has done little or nothing for dispersed shareholders. According to research conducted by Roger Martin, the dean of the Rotman School of Management, shareholders who invested in the S&P 500 in the years prior to 1976 enjoyed real compound average annual returns of 7.5 percent. After 1976, the average dropped to 6.5 percent, he says, and has been even lower than that since 2000.
#+end_quote
- Ch. 21. Stout's legal argument that shareholders own shares, not the company, so there is no legal duty to maximise share price
- Cites Roger Martin (Rotman School of Management): S&P 500 real compound returns of 7.5% a year before 1976, 6.5% after, lower still since 2000
